# Video Prompt Writing Guide (T2VA / I2VA / FL2VA / L2VA)

## 1. Task Overview

- **T2VA**: Builds a complete audiovisual timeline from text.
- **I2VA**: T2VA body + first-frame instruction + a visual path that develops forward from the first frame.
- **FL2VA**: T2VA body + first-and-last-frame instruction + a continuous path from the first frame to the last frame.
- **L2VA**: T2VA body + last-frame instruction + a path that converges from a plausible preceding state to the last frame.
- **Captioning a given multi-shot video**: a T2VA caption that describes an existing video of two or more shots joined by hard cuts precisely enough to regenerate it, with the same people, objects, and places from shot to shot. Follow Section 6.

## 2. Final Prompt Structure

### 2.1 Part One Is the Instruction

**T2VA** has no image-alignment instruction and begins directly with the three core fields.

**I2VA** always uses:

```text
For the target video, at 0.00 seconds into the target video, <Picture 1> (from [Shot 1]) is fully referenced.
```

**FL2VA** always uses:

```text
How the reference pictures align with the target video — Picture 1 (from Shot 1) aligns with the 0.00-second mark of the target video; Picture 2 (from Shot N) aligns with the S.SS-second mark of the target video.
```

**L2VA** always uses:

```text
How the reference pictures align with the target video — <Picture 1> (from [Shot N]) aligns with the S.SS-second mark of the target video.
```

Here, `N` is the index of the actual final shot, and `S.SS` is the effective video duration formatted to exactly two decimal places. The instruction must be the first line of the final prompt, followed by one blank line before the core fields.

### 2.2 Part Two Contains the Three Core Fields

```text
integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] ...

overall_soundscape: ...

non_diegetic_music: ...
```

- **integrated_multimodal_description**: Describes visuals, actions, shots, speakers, dialogue, singing, and diegetic audio along the timeline.
- **overall_soundscape**: Summarizes ambient sound, physical action sounds, and non-verbal human sounds across the entire video.
- **non_diegetic_music**: Describes background music that the characters cannot hear and only the audience can hear.

## 3. How to Incorporate Keyframes into the Multimodal Description

### 3.1 I2VA: Begin from the Image and Develop Forward

`<Picture 1>` is the actual first frame of the video at 0.00 seconds and belongs to `[Shot 1]`. The description should first establish the style, subjects, composition, and scene anchors in the image, then describe the next action. Character identity, clothing, colors, key objects, and spatial relationships should remain consistent.

Recommended structure: **first-frame anchor → action onset → continuous development → result or reaction**.

### 3.2 FL2VA: Describe the Path Between the First and Last Frames

Picture 1 is the opening, and Picture 2 is the ending. Focus on how the subject moves, how poses change, how objects are manipulated, how the composition evolves, and how the scene or lighting transitions.

FL2VA generally favors a single shot so the model can interpolate continuously from the first frame to the last frame. Use multiple shots only when they are explicitly specified. The last frame must be reached by the final `[Shot N]` at the end of the video.

Recommended structure: **first-frame state → observable intermediate changes → progressively narrowing differences → last-frame state**.

### 3.3 L2VA: Infer the Opening and Land on the Image at the End

`<Picture 1>` is the final frame of the video and belongs to the last `[Shot N]`; it does not inherently belong to Shot 1. Infer a plausible earlier state from the user's intent and the last frame, then describe how the characters, objects, camera, and scene gradually approach the reference image.

Recommended structure: **plausible preceding state → explicit action and transition path → gradual convergence in the final shot → last-frame landing**.

## 4. How to Write the Three Shared Core Sections

### 4.1 Develop the Multimodal Description Along the Timeline

`integrated_multimodal_description` is the main body of the rewritten prompt. Every detail should correspond to something visible or audible: visual style, initial composition, subject appearance and position, scene and key props, actions and reactions, shot changes, spoken language, and synchronized diegetic sound.

At the beginning of `[Shot 1]`, state the overall style and initial composition. Common styles include `Cinematic`, `live-action`, `2D-animated`, `3D CG`, `claymation`, `watercolor`, and `vintage film`. For keyframe tasks, derive the style from the reference image; for T2VA, select it from the user's text.

```text
[Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a medium-wide shot frames...
```

### 4.2 Shots and Cuts

Do not add a timestamp to the first shot. Use sequential shot numbers for later shots, and begin each one with a strictly increasing cut time that falls within the video duration:

```text
[Shot 2] At 00:03.500, the camera cuts to...
```

For ordinary cuts, use `the camera cuts to`, `the shot cuts to`, `the shot transitions to`, `the shot changes to`, or `the shot switches to`. When explicitly requested by the user, cross-dissolve, fade, or wipe may also be used. A cut should introduce new information about the subject, space, state, viewpoint, or time. If only the distance or a slight angle needs to change, prefer camera motion.

When a person, an animal, or a salient object from an earlier shot appears again, call it `the same` and repeat its key appearance in the same words each time, so its identity carries across the cut. A salient object is one the eye goes to or the action uses: a car or other vehicle, a phone, a weapon, a bag, a letter, a cup someone holds. For a person repeat hair and clothing; for an object its color, type, and distinctive marks. Then describe only what changed, such as a car door now open. A similar but different one is `another`:

```text
[Shot 3] At 00:06.500, the shot cuts back to the same young woman in the grey knit beanie and brown suede jacket, now leaning against the same red vintage convertible with chrome bumpers.
```

At every cut, also say what is behind the subjects now and how it differs from the previous shot: the same background, the other side of the same room and what is visible there, a closer view in which the background turns to a blur, a wider view that reveals more of the place, a new location, or a change in light, time of day, or weather. When a shot returns to a background shown in an earlier shot but not in the previous one, as in shot / reverse shot, say that it returns and repeat that background in the same words:

```text
[Shot 2] At 00:03.200, the camera cuts to a reverse angle on a man in a black leather jacket; behind him is now the rain-streaked front window of the diner instead of the chrome counter. [Shot 3] At 00:05.800, the shot cuts back to the same young woman in the grey knit beanie and brown suede jacket, the chrome counter of the diner behind her again.
```

### 4.3 Camera Motion: Motion Type + Amplitude + Speed

A complete camera-motion expression has three dimensions: the **motion type** defines how the camera moves, **amplitude** defines the range of compositional change, and **speed** defines the pacing of that change. Add amplitude and speed only when they are meaningful; medium amplitude and normal speed are usually omitted.

| Dimension | Available Expression | Description |
|-|-|-|
| Motion type | `Zoom In / Zoom Out` | The focal length changes while the camera body remains stationary |
| Motion type | `Push In / Pull Out` | The camera moves forward / backward |
| Motion type | `Pan Left / Pan Right` | The camera remains in place while the lens pivots horizontally |
| Motion type | `Truck Left / Truck Right` | The camera translates horizontally |
| Motion type | `Tilt Up / Tilt Down` | The camera remains in place while the lens pivots vertically |
| Motion type | `Pedestal Up / Pedestal Down` | The entire camera moves upward / downward |
| Motion type | `Arc Shot` | The camera moves in an arc around the subject |
| Motion type | `Tracking Shot` | The camera follows a moving subject |
| Motion type | `Static Shot` | The camera position and lens remain still |
| Motion type | `Shake Slightly / Shake Strongly` | Slight / strong camera shake |
| Motion type | `POV` | The subject's point of view |
| Motion type | `Roll Clockwise / Roll Counterclockwise` | The camera rolls clockwise / counterclockwise around the lens axis |
| Amplitude | `with small amplitude` | Small-range change |
| Amplitude | `with large amplitude` | Large-range change |
| Speed | `at slow speed` | Slow movement |
| Speed | `at fast speed` | Fast movement |

Camera motion should be written as a natural English action within the shot, rather than stacked as separate labels at the end of a sentence:

```text
The camera pushes in with small amplitude at slow speed toward the folded letter in her hands.
The camera pans right with large amplitude at fast speed, revealing the open doorway.
The camera holds a static shot as the runner exits the frame.
```

### 4.4 Speakers, Dialogue, and Singing

Subjects who speak, sing, or produce an off-screen human voice use stable IDs such as `(S1)` and `(S2)`. When multiple already-numbered speakers speak or sing together, use a compound ID such as `(S1,S2)`. A speaker keeps the same ID across shots; characters who never vocalize receive no speaker ID.

When a speaker first appears, provide enough information from the visual and audio context to establish a stable identity, such as character type, age, gender, whether the person is on-screen, pitch, timbre, speaking rate, or accent. Place the speaker's identifying phrase, ID, action, and delivery outside `<d>`. Inside `<d>`, include only the language tag and the actual user-provided spoken content. Preserve every original word and punctuation mark verbatim; do not translate or rewrite them.

```text
The young woman with a quiet, breathy voice (S1) says: <d>[English] I get off at the next station.</d>
The two children (S1,S2) shout together, <d>[English] Wait for us!</d>
```

For voiceover, use the exact phrase `says in an off-screen voiceover`. Immediately after every voiceover `<d>` block, state that the corresponding on-screen character's lips remain closed:

```text
The man (S1) says in an off-screen voiceover: <d>[English] I still remember that road.</d> while his lips remain completely closed.
```

When the same line of dialogue or lyrics crosses a cut, use `<scenetrans>` at the connecting points in both parts and explicitly state that the audio continues across the cut. Use `<cutoff>` when speech is truncated by the end of the video. Continuity may be expressed with `continues seamlessly across the cut`, `continues uninterrupted into the next shot`, `carries over from the previous shot`, or `remains audible across the transition`.

### 4.5 On-Screen Text

Place any banner, sign, label, subtitle, or neon text that is actually visible on screen in English double quotation marks. Preserve the original text and punctuation verbatim, without translation.

```text
A red neon sign reading "营业中" glows above the doorway.
```

### 4.6 overall_soundscape

Use 1–4 English sentences in one continuous paragraph to summarize the ambient sound, physical action sounds, and non-verbal human sounds across the full video, such as wind, rain, traffic, footsteps, fabric movement, impacts, breathing, laughter, or panting. Dialogue, singing, and diegetic music already belong in the multimodal description and should not be repeated here. Use `N/A` only when the user explicitly requests complete silence throughout the video.

```text
overall_soundscape: Steady rain taps against the café windows while low room ambience continues underneath. The entrance bell rings once, followed by wet footsteps and the soft scrape of a chair.
```

### 4.7 non_diegetic_music

Use 1–3 English sentences to describe background music that the characters cannot hear and only the audience can hear. Focus on instrumentation, speed, rhythm, and dynamic changes; do not use abstract mood words or explain the emotional function of the score. Singing, instruments, radio, television, or phone music audible to the characters are diegetic events and should appear in the multimodal description. Use `N/A` when there is no non-diegetic music.

```text
non_diegetic_music: Sparse piano notes at a slow tempo, joined by sustained low strings that gradually increase in volume before fading out.
```

## 5. Cases

### Case 1: T2VA

With no reference image, construct the complete timeline directly from the text. You may add scene, character, action, and sound details that remain consistent with the user's intent.

```text
integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a medium-wide shot frames a baker opening the shutters of a small street bakery before sunrise. The camera pushes in with small amplitude at slow speed as the middle-aged baker with a calm, slightly raspy voice (S1) places a fresh loaf on the wooden counter and says: <d>[English] First batch of the morning.</d> [Shot 2] At 00:05.000, the camera cuts to a close-up of steam rising from the sliced bread while the baker's final words carry over from the previous shot.

overall_soundscape: Wooden shutters scrape open over a quiet street as trays clink softly inside the bakery. The doorbell rings once, followed by light footsteps and the crisp sound of bread being sliced.

non_diegetic_music: A soft acoustic-guitar pattern at a moderate tempo, joined by sparse upright-bass notes and a gentle fade at the end.
```

### Case 2: I2VA

Write the first-frame instruction first, then use the subject, composition, and scene in Picture 1 as the starting point of Shot 1 before describing how the scene continues to develop.

```text
For the target video, at 0.00 seconds into the target video, <Picture 1> (from [Shot 1]) is fully referenced.

integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, the young woman shown in <Picture 1> remains beside the rain-covered train window, preserving her appearance, clothing, seat position, and the carriage layout. The camera trucks right with small amplitude at slow speed as she lifts her gaze from the folded letter toward the passing city lights. Her reflection moves across the glass while the quiet, breathy young woman (S1) says: <d>[English] I get off at the next station.</d> She folds the letter along its existing crease.

overall_soundscape: The train wheels produce a steady metallic rhythm beneath a low ventilation hum. Rain ticks against the window while paper rustles softly in her hands.

non_diegetic_music: Sustained cello notes at a slow tempo with widely spaced piano tones, gradually decreasing in volume.
```

### Case 3: FL2VA

The two images anchor the opening and ending respectively. The body should not repeat two static image descriptions; instead, it should supply the motion path that connects them. The following example is an eight-second single shot.

```text
How the reference pictures align with the target video — Picture 1 (from Shot 1) aligns with the 0.00-second mark of the target video; Picture 2 (from Shot 1) aligns with the 8.00-second mark of the target video.

integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a rain-soaked cyclist begins in the position and framing established by Picture 1, holding a closed black umbrella beside a silver bicycle. The camera pulls out with small amplitude at slow speed as she releases the bicycle handle, raises the umbrella above her shoulder, and presses the runner upward until the canopy opens. Water rolls from the expanding fabric while she steps beneath it, rotates the handle into the final angle, and settles into the pose, spacing, and composition established by Picture 2 at the end of the shot.

overall_soundscape: Rain falls steadily on the pavement, followed by the metallic click of the umbrella runner and the soft snap of the canopy opening. Water drips from the bicycle frame as distant traffic passes.

non_diegetic_music: N/A
```

### Case 4: L2VA

The image anchors only the final moment. First establish a compatible earlier state, then let the actions, object states, and composition gradually land on Picture 1 in the final shot. The following example is a six-second single shot.

```text
How the reference pictures align with the target video — <Picture 1> (from [Shot 1]) aligns with the 6.00-second mark of the target video.

integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a close shot begins with an intact drinking glass near the edge of a dark wooden table, while the same hand and sleeve visible in <Picture 1> approach from the right. The camera pushes in with small amplitude at slow speed as the fingertips strike the rim. The glass tips, falls, and hits the floor with a sharp impact; cracks spread through it as fragments slide outward. Toward the end, the moving pieces lose momentum and settle into the exact broken arrangement, hand position, camera angle, lighting, and final composition established by <Picture 1>.

overall_soundscape: Fingertips tap the glass before it scrapes across the tabletop, falls, and breaks with a sharp crash. Small fragments scatter and gradually stop sliding across the floor.

non_diegetic_music: A low electronic pulse at a slow tempo, ending immediately after the glass breaks.
```

## 6. Captioning a Given Multi-Shot Video

Use this section when you are given a video of two or more shots joined by hard cuts, usually a film or TV sequence, and asked for its caption. The caption is a T2VA prompt that describes what the video shows precisely enough to regenerate it, with the same people, objects, and places from shot to shot. It is written from the footage, not from a user's text. Where this section differs from Sections 2–4, this section applies; the styles, camera motion (4.3), and on-screen text (4.5) apply as written above. A video without a hard cut is a single shot: this section does not apply to it. Your answer is the finished caption and nothing else: no notes, lists, ids, or code fences, and nothing is added to or removed from it afterwards.

Work in two stages, and finish the first before you write a word of the caption:

1. **Stage 1, plan**: find the cuts and the start time of every shot (6.2), look at every shot, and decide who and what recurs across the shots and which background each shot shows (6.3).
2. **Stage 2, write**: write the caption from that plan (6.4–6.6), then check it (6.7) and correct what the check finds.

The plan is what keeps a recurring person or object named in identical words in every shot; deciding it while writing shot by shot is where names drift and links get missed.

### 6.1 Output

```text
integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] <style>, <framing> ... [Shot 2] At MM:SS.mmm, the camera cuts to ... [Shot 3] At MM:SS.mmm, the camera cuts back to ...

overall_soundscape: N/A

non_diegetic_music: N/A
```

- One `[Shot k]` per shot, numbered from 1 in order, all in one paragraph: the shots are separated by a single space, with no line breaks inside the description.
- `[Shot 1]` has no time. Every later shot starts with `At MM:SS.mmm, `, the start time of the shot (6.2), followed by a cut phrase in lower case.
- The caption describes the picture only. Both sound fields are exactly `N/A`. Write no dialogue (`<d>`), no speaker IDs, and no sound, music, or voice words in the description; a person whose lips move may be described as speaking. No first-frame or last-frame instruction line.

### 6.2 Stage 1: Find the Shots and Their Start Times

A shot runs from one hard cut to the next; camera moves and fast action inside a shot are not cuts. Shot k starts at its first frame: the time is the index of that frame (counting from 0) divided by the frame rate, written as minutes and seconds with three decimals, rounded to the millisecond. For example, frame 31 at 24000/1001 fps is 1.293 seconds, `00:01.293`. When the cuts are given, use them as they are and do not detect them again: as the first frame index of every shot after the first (shot k+1 starts at the k-th index / fps), or as shot lengths in frames (shot k starts at the frames of shots 1 to k-1 / fps). The times are strictly increasing and below the video's duration.

With a shell, ffmpeg gives the frame rate and frame count, and ranks the frames by how much they differ from the frame before:

```bash
ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 -count_frames -show_entries stream=r_frame_rate,nb_read_frames -of default=nw=1 in.mp4
ffmpeg -hide_banner -i in.mp4 -vf "select='gte(scene,0)',metadata=print:file=-" -an -f null - 2>/dev/null \
  | awk '/^frame:/{f=$1; t=$3} /scene_score/{split($0,a,"="); print a[2], f, t}' | sort -g -r | head -20
```

The second command prints score, `frame:<index>`, and `pts_time:<seconds>`. Cuts stand far above the other frames, but their scores vary with the footage: in dark footage they can be 0.11–0.14 against below 0.02 for every other frame, so a fixed threshold such as 0.3 misses them. A flash, a whip pan, or fast motion can also score high. Look at the frames on both sides of every candidate before calling it a cut.

To watch the video, extract frames, for example 4 per second at 448 pixels high (file N shows the time (N-1)/4 seconds), and contact sheets of 30 frames at 2 per second for comparing shots side by side:

```bash
mkdir -p frames && ffmpeg -hide_banner -loglevel error -i in.mp4 -vf "fps=4,scale=-2:448" frames/%04d.jpg
ffmpeg -hide_banner -loglevel error -i in.mp4 -vf "fps=2,scale=-2:180,tile=6x5" sheet_%02d.jpg
```

Look at least at the first, middle, and last frames of every shot, and at the frames on both sides of every cut.

### 6.3 Stage 1: Decide Who and What Recurs, and the Backgrounds

Decide these before you write. None of it appears in the answer as a list; it decides the wording of the texts. Compare the shots side by side (a contact sheet helps) rather than one after another.

- **Entities**: every person, animal, and salient object seen in more than one shot, and the shots that show each. A salient object is one the eye goes to or the action uses: a vehicle, a phone, a weapon, a bag or suitcase, a letter or photo, a cup or bottle someone holds, furniture someone sits at or leans on, a sign or screen. A recurring salient object counts as much as a person. Someone or something seen in one shot only is not an entity: describe it as usual in that shot.
- **The very same one**: link two shots only on visible evidence: the same face, hair, build, and clothing, or the same color, shape, make, and markings. An edit pattern (a reverse angle, a point-of-view shot, an insert) or a similar color is not enough. When you are unsure, look again at full resolution before deciding: crop the head or the object from frames of both shots (mirror one when they face opposite ways) and compare them side by side; a profile and a three-quarter view can make the same face look narrower. When you still cannot tell, treat them as two; never merge two different people or objects. A similar but different one (a second black phone, another man in a suit) is its own entity.
- **Anchors**: give each entity an anchor, its appearance as it is in every shot that shows it: 5–14 words, lower case (except on-screen text or a brand name), without an article. A person: apparent age and gender, hair, the most visible clothing (`young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie and brown suede jacket`). An animal: species, color, markings (`black-and-white border collie with a red collar`). An object: color, type or make, material, a distinctive mark (`red vintage convertible with chrome bumpers`). An anchor never holds an action, pose, position, or place (`handing envelopes`, `seated at the far right`, `held in one hand`, `on the table`): those change from shot to shot. Two entities never get the same or a near-identical anchor; name what tells them apart (`bearded man in the grey overcoat`, `bald man in the black sweater`).
- **Backgrounds**: every distinct background seen behind the subjects, each with an anchor: the place and the part of it in view (`bookshelf wall of a wood-paneled study`, `rain-streaked window side of the study`). Shots in front of the same background (the same wall, window, or stretch of street) share it even when the framing differs; the other side of a room, or a view of a different part of the place, is another background. Note which background each shot shows.

### 6.4 Stage 2: Name Every Entity the Same Way in Every Shot

- In the first shot that shows an entity, write `a` or `an` + its anchor, where its noun phrase goes: `A young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie steps in from the left`.
- In every later shot that shows it, write `the same` + its anchor, word for word, at its first mention in that shot, also when it is seen from behind, from another side, or only in part, and also after shots without it. Later mentions in the same shot are a pronoun or a short noun (`she`, `the car`).
- The anchor phrase is a complete noun phrase. Keep the anchor's words out of what surrounds it: no adjective of the anchor in front of it (`a brown a brown leather backpack`), no restatement after it (`the same woman with long dark hair, a woman with long dark hair,`), and no repeat of its last words (`a round wooden table heaped with books heaped with books`). What follows the anchor phrase is only what is new in this shot: action, position, framing, or state (`the same young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie, now seen from the front, grips the wheel`; `the same red vintage convertible with chrome bumpers, its driver's door now open`).
- Do not put a possessive on the anchor phrase: `the hand of the same young woman with ...`, not `the same young woman with ...'s hand`.
- `The same` is only for something already described in an earlier shot: never in Shot 1 and never at an entity's first appearance. Never a bare `the same man`, `the same woman`, or `the same car`. Someone or something that first appears in a later shot is `a` or `another` + its description.
- State a link through the wording only; do not explain how you know it (`confirming`, `revealing it is`, `identifiable as`).

With the anchors `young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie` and `red vintage convertible with chrome bumpers`:

```text
[Shot 1] ... A young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie walks along the curb toward a red vintage convertible with chrome bumpers and opens its door. [Shot 2] At 00:04.171, the camera cuts to a close-up of the same young woman with a blonde bun in a grey knit beanie, now seen through the windshield, as she grips the wheel.
```

### 6.5 Stage 2: Say What Is Behind the Subjects at Every Cut

- Shot 1 describes its background: the place, what is behind the subjects, the light.
- Every later shot says, right after its framing, what is behind its subjects now compared with the previous shot:
  - the same background as the previous shot: `against the same bookshelf wall of the study`;
  - a different one: what is now visible and what is no longer there (`the reverse angle shows the rain-streaked window behind him instead of the bookshelves`), a closer view in which the background turns to a blur, or a wider view that reveals more of the place;
  - a background seen in an earlier shot but not in the previous one, as in shot / reverse shot: it returns. Say so with `back` or `again` and `the same` + its anchor: `the camera cuts back to the man, the same bookshelf wall of a wood-paneled study behind him again`.
- Also state visible changes of light, time of day, weather, or set between shots (a lamp now on, a door now open, rain starting). Keep the style and color grade consistent and mention them again only when they change.

### 6.6 Stage 2: Write Each Shot

- 30–110 words of fluent English per shot, depending on how much happens: the framing and composition, the subjects and where they are, the background, the action from the start to the end of the shot, and the camera motion.
- Shot 1 starts with the style and then the framing: `Live-action, cinematic, a static medium shot frames ...`. Styles: `Live-action, cinematic`; `Live-action, documentary`; `Live-action, handheld realism`; `vintage film`; `black-and-white film`; `2D-animated`; `3D CG`; `stop-motion`. Use `Live-action, cinematic` for fiction film and TV drama or comedy, and `Live-action, documentary` for reality TV, interviews, talks, nature, sports, and event footage.
- Every later shot starts, after its time, with a cut phrase in lower case: `the camera cuts to`, `the camera cuts back to`, `the shot changes to`, `the shot switches to`. Use `cuts back to` only for a camera setup, subject, or background shown in an earlier shot.
- Camera motion as in 4.3, judged from the background, not from the subjects; when the frame does not move, `the camera holds a static shot`.
- Quote clearly legible on-screen text verbatim in double quotes (4.5).
- Only what is visible: no names, no story or intentions, no emotions stated as facts (describe the visible expression). No shot numbers, timestamps (except quoted on-screen clocks), or remarks about the video or the captioning inside the texts.
- Describe what changes, not what is already known: an entity's appearance is in its anchor, so a later shot adds only its pose, position, action, framing, and what is new.
- Notes that come with the video (earlier captions, per-shot descriptions, cut labels) help you notice details but can be wrong, and their wording may not follow these rules: write only what the video shows, in the form of this section.

### 6.7 Stage 2: Check Before Answering

1. One `[Shot k]` per shot, in order; Shot 1 without a time; every later shot `At MM:SS.mmm, ` with its start time, then a lower-case cut phrase; one paragraph; then the two `N/A` fields.
2. Every entity is named in every shot that shows it: `a` or `an` + its anchor in its first shot, and `the same` + the identical anchor in every later one. Compare the words of each anchor across shots.
3. No anchor words doubled around a named entity, no `the same the same`, no bare `the same man` / `woman` / `car`, and no `the same` in Shot 1.
4. Every later shot says what is behind its subjects; every return says `back` or `again` with `the same` + the background's anchor.
5. No sound, music, voice, or dialogue words; no names; no braces, ids, or notes; each shot 30–110 words.

### 6.8 Example

A three-shot sequence at 24000/1001 fps whose shots are 63, 196, and 93 frames long (cuts at frames 63 and 259). Entities: the young woman (shots 1–3) and the lantern (shots 1 and 3); the two women in shot 2 appear in that shot only. Backgrounds: the balcony (shots 1 and 3, a return) and the courtyard below (shot 2).

```text
integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a static medium shot frames an exterior balcony with blurred sunlit green trees filling the background. A rusty metal hanging lantern with glass panels hangs in the foreground center.
```
